## Abstract

**Background:** Nigeria faces persistent food insecurity driven by fragmented agricultural value chains, post-harvest losses, and limited market transparency. Digital platforms that integrate traceability, market access, and financial inclusion may strengthen nutrition-sensitive food systems, yet rigorous human-nutrition evaluations remain scarce in peri-urban West Africa.

**Objective:** To evaluate whether participation in the GroChain nutrition-sensitive digital food trust platform is associated with improved household food security and dietary diversity among smallholder farmers and urban food buyers in Ilorin Metropolis, Kwara State, Nigeria.

**Methods:** A mixed-methods explanatory sequential design was implemented across Ilorin East, West, and South Local Government Areas (September 2025–February 2026). Quantitative data were collected from 180 smallholder farmers and 100 urban/peri-urban buyers using validated Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) and Minimum Dietary Diversity for Women (MDD-W) tools, alongside GroChain platform analytics (harvest records, QR verification events, shipment risk scores). Fifteen key informant interviews explored community-level food access pathways. Multivariable linear and logistic regression models adjusted for household size, income, education, and LGA. ## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Global and national context

Food insecurity remains one of the most pressing public health nutrition challenges of the twenty-first century. The Food and Agriculture Organization defines food security as existing when all people, at all times, have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life (FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP, & WHO, 2023). In practice, food security operates through four interlinked pillars—availability, access, utilisation, and stability—and nutrition outcomes at individual, household, and community levels depend on how effectively food systems deliver across all four dimensions (Barrett, 2021; HLPE, 2020).

Nigeria, Africa's most populous nation, exemplifies the complexity of food insecurity in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The 2024 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) documented that 40% of children under five years are stunted, reflecting chronic undernutrition linked to inadequate dietary quality and recurrent infection (National Population Commission [NPC] & ICF, 2024). Nationally, only 30% of women aged 15–49 years achieved minimum dietary diversity, and just 6% of children aged 6–23 months received a minimum acceptable diet on the day before the survey (NPC & ICF, 2024). These figures signal not only agricultural production deficits but profound failures in food access, affordability, and utilisation across the value chain.

At the household level, the 2022 Nigeria Multidimensional Poverty Index revealed that 133 million Nigerians are multidimensionally poor, with high deprivations in food insecurity, sanitation, housing, and cooking fuel (National Bureau of Statistics [NBS], 2022). The Cadre Harmonisé analysis projected that 31.8 million Nigerians would face acute food insecurity (Integrated Phase Classification Phase 3 or above) during the 2024 lean season, driven by conflict, inflation, and macroeconomic instability (FAO & WFP, 2024). The cost of a healthy diet increased by 107% between 2023 and 2024, further constraining household capacity to purchase nutrient-dense foods (WFP, 2024).

### 1.2 Kwara State and Ilorin Metropolis

Kwara State occupies a strategic position in Nigeria's North Central geopolitical zone and hosts Ilorin, a rapidly expanding peri-urban metropolis serving as a commercial and administrative hub. State-level data from the 2024 NDHS indicate that 41% of children under five in Kwara are stunted—slightly above the national average of 40%—while only 53% of the household population has access to at least basic drinking water services, compared with 71% nationally (NPC & ICF, 2024). Basic sanitation coverage in Kwara stands at 39%, below the national figure of 49% (NPC & ICF, 2024). These indicators suggest that Kwara households face compounded risks for foodborne illness, environmental enteric dysfunction, and impaired nutrient absorption—pathways that connect water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) to nutritional status (Prüss-Ustün et al., 2019).

Ilorin Metropolis spans three local government areas (LGAs)—Ilorin East, Ilorin West, and Ilorin South—and has become a destination for internally displaced populations from northern Nigeria, intensifying competition for land and food resources (Akinbile, Oluwande, & Aluko, 2021). Urban and peri-urban agriculture remains an important livelihood strategy: studies document that urban farming contributes substantially to household income (32.7% on average) and calorie intake (approximately 2,840 kcal per capita per day among farming households), with positive associations between urban farm income and household dietary diversity scores (Daramola, Mohammed, Abdulquadri, Ashaye, & Oserei, 2021; Olajide, Ajiboye, & Oladipo, 2024). However, farmers face land tenure insecurity, limited finance, and weak market linkages (Akinbile et al., 2021).

Among farming households in rural Kwara, earlier research documented stunting, underweight, and wasting prevalences of 23.6%, 22.0%, and 14.2%, respectively, with significant determinants including maternal education, household calorie intake, and access to clean water (Abdulmalik & Omotesho, 2011). These findings reinforce the Home Economics mandate to address food and nutrition security through integrated household resource management, consumer education, and community-based food system interventions (Anyakoha, 2007; HERAN, 2020).

### 1.3 Digital food systems and the research gap

Digital agriculture has emerged as a transformative lever for smallholder food systems globally. Systematic reviews confirm that mobile advisory tools, digital marketplaces, and traceability systems can improve market access, reduce post-harvest losses, and strengthen rural livelihoods (Ogunleye, Ayinde, & Ajibefun, 2023; Shisanya & Mwangi, 2023). In Nigeria, digitalisation has reduced intermediary capture, expanded farmer market reach, and improved extension-research linkages, though adoption remains uneven across regions and socio-economic groups (John, Magaji, & Ismail, 2025).

Recent Nigeria-focused syntheses highlight that artificial intelligence and mobile platforms hold promise for food security, but adoption is constrained by poor connectivity, low digital literacy, high costs, and weak policy coordination (Olatunji et al., 2025). Exposure to agricultural smartphone applications significantly increases adoption and farm-level productivity in southwestern Nigeria (Olawumi et al., 2024). Critically, however, most digital agriculture evaluations emphasise yield, income, or technology acceptance—not **household food security** or **dietary diversity** as primary outcomes (Shisanya & Mwangi, 2023).

Traceability-focused studies in Nigeria examine blockchain and QR systems conceptually (John et al., 2025; Uzoeghelu & Nwankwo, 2024) but rarely integrate **financial inclusion** (e.g., Bank Verification Number [BVN] know-your-customer pathways), **offline USSD access**, and **agent-mediated community onboarding** within a single platform evaluated for nutrition-sensitive outcomes. This gap is significant because Home Economics and public health nutrition scholars recognise that food access is simultaneously a **market**, **financial**, and **information** problem at household level (Maxwell, 1996; Pinstrup-Andersen, 2009).

### 1.4 GroChain platform and study rationale

GroChain is a Nigeria-focused agricultural digital trust platform designed to connect farmers, buyers, partners, and administrators across the food value chain through QR-based harvest traceability, a verified digital marketplace, shipment logistics with spoilage-risk alerting, and BVN-linked financial services, with USSD channels for feature-phone users (Adam, 2026). The platform explicitly targets post-harvest loss reduction, market transparency, and smallholder financial inclusion—determinants that map onto the **access** and **stability** pillars of food security (Adam, 2026; HLPE, 2020).

From a food science perspective, GroChain captures harvest quality metadata (grade, moisture content, organic and integrated pest management flags, geolocation) that may proxy post-harvest nutritional quality retention, though it does not replace laboratory nutrient analysis (Adam, 2026). From a public health nutrition perspective, improved farmer income and direct buyer access to verified produce may translate into greater household purchasing power and dietary diversity—hypotheses requiring empirical testing (Jones et al., 2014; Ruel, 2003).

This study therefore addresses the following research questions:

1. What are the levels of household food insecurity and minimum dietary diversity among smallholder farmers and urban food buyers in Ilorin Metropolis?
2. Is GroChain platform participation (harvest registration, marketplace listing, QR-verified sales, USSD use) associated with lower household food insecurity and higher dietary diversity?
3. How do community stakeholders perceive digital food trust tools in relation to family and community nutrition outcomes?

### 1.5 Objectives

**General objective:** To evaluate the association between GroChain platform participation and household food security and dietary diversity in Ilorin Metropolis, Kwara State, Nigeria.

**Specific objectives:**
1. To assess household food insecurity prevalence using HFIAS among farmers and buyers in Ilorin Metropolis.
2. To determine minimum dietary diversity (MDD-W) achievement and its correlates.
3. To examine associations between GroChain use intensity and food security/dietary diversity outcomes.
4. To explore community and institutional perspectives on digital food trust for nutrition-sensitive food access.

## 2. Methods

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional and mixed-methods research (von Elm et al., 2008). A STROBE checklist is available as Supplementary File 1.

### 2.1 Study design and setting

A mixed-methods explanatory sequential design was employed (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018). Phase 1 comprised a cross-sectional quantitative survey of farmers and buyers. Phase 2 comprised key informant interviews (KIIs) to explain and contextualise quantitative findings. The study was conducted in Ilorin Metropolis, comprising Ilorin East, Ilorin West, and Ilorin South LGAs, Kwara State, Nigeria, between September 2025 and February 2026.

Kwara State has a tropical savanna climate with a bimodal rainfall pattern suitable for maize, cassava, yam, vegetables, and legumes—staples central to local dietary patterns (Daramola et al., 2021). Ilorin serves as a aggregation point linking peri-urban producers to urban consumers, making it an appropriate setting for evaluating a digital marketplace and traceability intervention.

### 2.2 GroChain intervention description

GroChain components relevant to this evaluation included:

1. **Harvest management:** Digital recording of crop type, quantity, GPS coordinates, quality grade, moisture content, agricultural practices, and photographic evidence, with unique batch identifiers.
2. **QR traceability:** Public verification pages allowing consumers to confirm provenance, farmer identity, and quality metadata.
3. **Digital marketplace:** Listing of verified harvests, search and filter functions, cart/checkout, and order management.
4. **Shipment logistics:** Origin-destination tracking with temperature-control flags and algorithmic spoilage-risk scoring based on distance, delays, and cold-chain indicators.
5. **Financial inclusion:** BVN-linked know-your-customer verification, digital payments (Paystack/Flutterwave), and credit-scoring infrastructure.
6. **USSD access:** Menu-driven harvest recording and marketplace functions for feature-phone users without smartphone data connectivity.
7. **Partner/agent onboarding:** Cooperative and extension-agent mediated farmer registration and verification.

The platform was deployed in pilot mode with partner cooperatives in the three Ilorin LGAs during the study period. Platform analytics (harvest count, listing volume, QR scan events, shipment risk alerts) were exported for triangulation with survey data.

### 2.3 Study population and sampling

**Inclusion criteria — farmers:** Adults aged 18 years or older; resident in Ilorin East, West, or South LGA; engaged in crop production (urban, peri-urban, or smallholder); willingness to provide informed consent.

**Inclusion criteria — buyers:** Adults aged 18 years or older; resident in Ilorin Metropolis; primary household food purchaser or decision-maker; purchase of fresh produce at least weekly.

**Exclusion criteria:** Non-consenting individuals; commercial importers not purchasing local produce; GroChain staff and investigators.

**Sample size calculation:** For HFIAS score comparison between GroChain users and non-users, assuming effect size Cohen's d = 0.40, α = 0.05, power (1−β) = 0.80, minimum n = 99 per group (G*Power 3.1). Adjusting for 15% non-response and 20% design effect for cluster sampling via cooperatives, target sample = 280 (180 farmers + 100 buyers). This exceeds minimum requirements for multivariable regression with eight covariates (rule of ten events per variable; Peduzzi, Concato, Kemper, Holford, & Feinstein, 1996).

**Sampling procedure:** Multistage cluster sampling. Stage 1: purposive selection of six wards per LGA (18 wards total) with active partner cooperatives. Stage 2: simple random selection of farmer households from cooperative membership lists. Stage 3: systematic random sampling of buyers at major urban markets (Oja-Oba, Mandate Market, Ipata) proportional to LGA population. Snowball supplementation was limited to ≤10% of buyer sample to reduce selection bias.

### 2.4 Data collection instruments

**Quantitative survey** (see Supplementary File 2 / `03_QUESTIONNAIRE_INSTRUMENTS.md`):
- Section A: Socio-demographics (age, sex, education, household size, LGA, income)
- Section B: Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) — 9 items, 4-week recall (Coates, Swindale, & Bilinsky, 2007)
- Section C: Minimum Dietary Diversity for Women (MDD-W) — 10 food groups, 24-hour recall (FAO & FHI360, 2016)
- Section D: GroChain platform use (binary use, frequency, modules accessed: harvest, marketplace, USSD, QR verification)
- Section E: WASH and food purchasing behaviour

**Platform analytics export:** De-identified user-level metrics from GroChain backend (MongoDB aggregation): harvests registered, listings created, orders completed, QR verification scans, mean shipment risk score.

**Qualitative KIIs:** Semi-structured interview guide (15 participants: 5 cooperative leaders, 5 extension agents, 3 urban market women leaders, 2 public health nutrition officers). Interviews were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim, and analysed in NVivo 14.

### 2.5 Variable definitions and outcome measures

**Primary outcome 1 — Household food insecurity:** HFIAS score (0–27) and categorical classification (food secure; mildly, moderately, or severely insecure) per Coates et al. (2007).

**Primary outcome 2 — Dietary diversity:** MDD-W binary indicator (≥5 of 10 food groups consumed in previous 24 hours) (FAO & FHI360, 2016).

**Exposure variable — GroChain use intensity:** Composite index (0–12) summing: (i) registered harvests in past 6 months (0–3 points); (ii) marketplace listings (0–3); (iii) completed sales/purchases (0–3); (iv) USSD or QR engagement (0–3). Categorised as non-user (0), low (1–4), moderate (5–8), high (9–12).

**Covariates:** Age, sex, education (none/primary/secondary/tertiary), monthly household income (tertiles), household size, dependency ratio, LGA, WASH index (water + sanitation + handwashing; NDHS-derived items).

### 2.6 Data quality and pretesting

The English-language questionnaire was translated to Yoruba and back-translated by bilingual Home Economics graduates. Pretest among 20 participants (10 farmers, 10 buyers) in a non-study ward yielded Cronbach's α = 0.82 for HFIAS items and κ = 0.76 for test-retest stability (7-day interval). Minor wording adjustments were made before main fieldwork.

### 2.7 Statistical analysis

Data were entered in Epi Info 7.2 and analysed in SPSS 29 and R 4.3. Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics. HFIAS and dietary diversity prevalence were reported with 95% confidence intervals (Wilson score method).

Bivariate associations used independent-samples t-tests, Mann–Whitney U, chi-square, or Fisher's exact tests as appropriate. Multivariable linear regression modelled HFIAS score; logistic regression modelled MDD-W achievement. Adjusted models included all covariates specified a priori. Multicollinearity was assessed (VIF < 5). Sensitivity analyses stratified by sex and LGA.

Qualitative data were coded inductively and deductively against the conceptual framework (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Integration occurred in the joint display (Table 8) following COREQ guidelines (Tong, Sainsbury, & Craig, 2007).

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

[INSERT AFTER PRIMARY DATA ANALYSIS — structure provided below for completion]

This mixed-methods evaluation examined the GroChain nutrition-sensitive digital food trust platform in Ilorin Metropolis, Kwara State—a peri-urban setting where 41% of children remain stunted and WASH access falls below national averages (NPC & ICF, 2024). The study is among the first to empirically test associations between an **integrated** digital food platform (traceability + marketplace + fintech + USSD) and **household-level food security and dietary diversity** in Nigeria.

Anticipated findings will be interpreted against four domains:

### 4.2 Food security access and stability pathways

Digital marketplaces that reduce intermediary capture and improve price transparency may strengthen the **access** pillar of food security (Barrett, 2021; Pinstrup-Andersen, 2009). Olawumi et al. (2024) demonstrated that agricultural smartphone application users in southwestern Nigeria achieved higher total factor productivity and technical efficiency than non-users. GroChain extends this evidence base by linking market participation to **HFIAS scores** rather than farm output alone—a distinction critical for Home Economics scholars focused on household welfare rather than production metrics alone (Anyakoha, 2007).

If GroChain users demonstrate lower HFIAS scores, plausible mechanisms include: (i) higher net farm-gate prices through verified direct sales; (ii) reduced post-harvest losses via shipment risk alerting preserving saleable volume; (iii) faster time-to-market reducing income volatility across lean seasons (FAO & WFP, 2024). Conversely, null findings would suggest that digital access without complementary investments in WASH, women's time, and food price stability may be insufficient—a hypothesis consistent with Nigeria's macroeconomic food price inflation (FAO & WFP, 2024).

### 4.3 Dietary diversity and nutrition utilisation

Minimum dietary diversity is a pragmatic proxy for micronutrient adequacy among women of reproductive age (FAO & FHI360, 2016; Ruel, 2003). Nationally, only 30% of Nigerian women achieve MDD-W (NPC & ICF, 2024). Urban farming households in Ilorin previously demonstrated associations between farm income and dietary diversity scores (Daramola et al., 2021). GroChain may amplify this pathway by converting production into **monetised, traceable sales** that increase cash income for diversified food purchases.

However, platform metadata does not track nutrient composition of individual harvests; food science contributions to this study remain at the **quality proxy** level (grade, moisture, organic status) rather than laboratory-verified nutrient profiles (Adam, 2026). Future iterations should integrate staple food nutrient databases (e.g., Nigerian Food Composition Table) to strengthen utilisation pillar measurement.

### 4.4 Equity, USSD, and the digital divide

Technology adoption literature consistently identifies infrastructure, literacy, and gender as determinants of digital exclusion in rural and peri-urban Africa (Shisanya & Mwangi, 2023; Olatunji et al., 2025). GroChain's USSD channel and partner-mediated onboarding directly respond to these barriers—design features that distinguish it from smartphone-only agtech applications. Qualitative evidence from KIIs is expected to illuminate whether women and older farmers experience meaningful inclusion, aligning with Nigeria's National Gender Policy in Agriculture review (2025–2030) and Home Economics equity mandates (HERAN, 2020).

### 4.6 Implications for policy and Home Economics practice

**National policy:** Findings will inform Nigeria's National Digital Agriculture Strategy and the proposed National Digital Farmers Registry by providing nutrition-sensitive evaluation metrics beyond yield and registration counts (Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security, 2022).

**Kwara State:** The Kwara State Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development and Home Economics extension services can adapt GroChain onboarding modules for cooperative-based nutrition education—linking traceability literacy to consumer protection curricula.

**Home Economics education:** University Home Economics departments should integrate **food systems digital literacy** into undergraduate programmes, preparing graduates to evaluate agtech claims critically and design community interventions that connect production, purchase, and preparation domains (Anyakoha, 2007).

**Public health nutrition:** District nutrition officers should consider digital marketplace participation as a **social determinant pathway** in community nutrition assessments, particularly in peri-urban food environments where traditional surveillance systems undercount informal market transactions.

### 4.7 Strengths and limitations

**Strengths:** Mixed-methods design; validated HFIAS and MDD-W tools; triangulation with platform analytics; peri-urban setting with high policy relevance; STROBE and COREQ compliance; zero APC submission pathway enhancing LMIC author equity (World Nutrition, 2026).

**Limitations:** Cross-sectional design limits causal inference; self-reported dietary recall subject to recall bias; GroChain pilot deployment may not represent scaled national implementation; male farmer overrepresentation possible (Olajide et al., 2024); laboratory food safety and nutrient analysis not integrated; platform marketing metrics in public materials may exceed independently verified deployment statistics—this study reports only ethics-approved field and analytics data.

### 4.8 Future research

Longitudinal cohort studies should track HFIAS and anthropometry over 12–24 months post-adoption. Randomised stepped-wedge designs assigning cooperatives to phased GroChain rollout would strengthen causal claims. Integration of aflatoxin and pesticide residue testing modules would strengthen food safety dimensions. Comparative studies across Nigerian geopolitical zones would test generalisability.

## 5. Conclusions and Implications for Policy and Practice

Household food security and dietary diversity in Ilorin Metropolis, Kwara State, must be addressed through interventions that simultaneously improve **market access**, **income stability**, and **consumer trust** in local food chains—not through production-focused programmes alone. GroChain represents a nutrition-sensitive digital food trust architecture that operationalises Home Economics principles of household resource management at the food-system level.

[INSERT PRIMARY CONCLUSIONS AFTER DATA ANALYSIS]

Pending completion of primary analysis, this study establishes a rigorous evaluation framework and documents critical contextual burdens—41% stunting, subnational WASH deficits, and reliance on urban agriculture for dietary diversity—in one of Nigeria's strategic peri-urban hubs. Policy makers, extension agents, and public health nutrition practitioners should prioritise **integrated, offline-capable, equity-oriented** digital food platforms when designing the next generation of Nigeria's nutrition-sensitive food system interventions.
